SleepPACNet: new convolutional 
neural network considering 
phase-amplitude coupling 
for automatic sleep stage 
classification using single-channel 
electroencephalogram
Taegyeong Lee1, Hyerin Nam2, JangJay Sohn1 & Chang-Hwan Im1,2,3
Automatic sleep stage classification (ASSC) using single-channel electroencephalogram (EEG) 
signals is an essential technology for developing wearable EEG devices for sleep monitoring. 
Deep learning (DL)-based methods, although effective in automatically extracting features, often 
overlook significant EEG characteristics containing domain-specific knowledge relevant to particular 
sleep stages, such as cross-frequency phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) phenomena. To address this 
limitation, we proposed SleepPACNet, a novel convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture 
specifically designed to effectively extract PAC-related features from single-channel EEG signals to 
enhance ASSC performance. Central to our proposed model is a PAC feature extraction module, which 
employs a CNN layer explicitly designed to integrate the phase information from low-frequency EEG 
components, obtained using the Hilbert transform, with amplitude information from high-frequency 
EEG components. The performance of SleepPACNet was evaluated using single-channel (Fp1–Fp2) 
sleep EEG data extracted from the Dreem dataset to simulate conditions of a portable EEG device 
measuring prefrontal bipolar EEG signals. SleepPACNet outperformed conventional CNN models, 
including SleepCNN, achieving the highest classification accuracy of 75.7%. It significantly improved 
REM stage classification, validating PAC’s effectiveness in REM detection. These findings highlight the 
potential of automated PAC feature extraction in DL for improving the overall performance of ASSC 
systems utilizing single-channel EEG.
Keywords Automatic sleep stage classification (ASSC), Electroencephalography (EEG), Phase-amplitude 
coupling, Hilbert transform, Convolutional neural network (CNN)
Sleep is an important human activity for maintaining both physical and mental health in humans 1,2. During 
sleep, the stages of sleep change according to the brain’s state 3. Sleep stages are generally classified into the 
following five stages: wake (W), rapid eyes movements (REM), and three non-REM stages (referred to as N1, N2, 
and N3)4. Identifying these sleep stages is essential for the diagnosis of sleep disorders and measurement of sleep 
quality5,6. Sleep experts typically perform manual scoring of the sleep stages based on established guidelines, such 
as the Rechtschaffen and Kales (R&K) and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), typically using 
polysomnography (PSG), which encompasses various biomedical signals, including electroencephalography 
(EEG), electromyography (EMG), and electrooculography (EOG) 7,8. However, this manual scoring method 
requires considerable effort and time investment from sleep experts, and the excessive workload can sometimes 
reduce the accuracy and reliability of sleep stage scoring9,10. In addition, attaching a large number of sensors and 
sleeping in an unfamiliar environment can cause discomfort for participants, and the measurement procedure

devices capable of conveniently diagnosing sleep disorders or assessing sleep quality in home environments.
Among the biomedical signals utilized in PSG, EEG is considered the most critical modality for sleep-stage 
scoring due to its rich neurophysiological information 12. This characteristic has prompted numerous ASSC 
studies to focus exclusively on EEG signals. Specifically, since scalp electrodes attached to hairy area may interfere 
with natural sleep13, ASSC based on traditional machine learning (ML) methods using single-channel prefrontal 
EEG recordings has attracted considerable attention as a means to minimize the number of required electrodes. 
For instance, Liang et al. 14 employed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to classify sleep stages by extracting 
handcrafted features, including autoregressive coefficients and multiscale entropy. Handcrafted features used 
for traditional ML-based ASSC are advantageous because they can explicitly incorporate domain knowledge 15, 
thereby making the classification process interpretable 16. However, the use of handcrafted features may limit 
classification performance due to potential loss of information necessary for classification17.
Recently, many studies have demonstrated the potential of deep learning (DL)-based ASSC using single-
channel EEG, which can automatically extract features from raw EEG signals with minimal information loss. 
Particularly, most DL-based ASSC studies have employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which showed 
exceptional performance in feature extraction from EEG. For instance, Goshtasbi et al. 18 introduced a CNN 
architecture that integrates a feature extraction module with a temporal sequence learning module. Mousavi et 
al.19 developed the SleepEEGNet, consisting of CNN for extracting features from time-frequency information 
of EEG and a sequence-to-sequence model for recognizing contextual dependencies between continuous 
EEG signals. Additionally, the TinySleepNet, proposed by Supratak et al. 20, also includes a CNN-based feature 
extraction module. However, while CNN-based feature extractors effectively capture patterns from raw EEG 
data, they often underperform due to limited generalization and insufficient domain knowledge. With recent 
advancements in large language models, large-scale foundation models for EEG, such as EEGPT21, NeuroGPT22, 
LaBram23, and BCINetV124, have been developed to improve generalization through pre-training on extensive 
datasets. Alongside these foundation models, recent multidomain frameworks have also been proposed to achieve 
cross-discipline adaptability and robust generalization 25. Despite their enhanced generalization, these models 
may not fully leverage some domain knowledge that was successfully embedded in conventional handcrafted 
features15,16, highlighting the need for further model development in this regard. Therefore, incorporation of 
specific domain knowledge into DL-based ASSC has the potential to increase classification performance, as 
shown in previous DL-based EEG studies that attempted to incorporate handcrafted features into DL in other 
EEG classification problems26–28.
Among the diverse handcrafted EEG features employed for traditional ML-based ASSC using single-channel 
EEG, cross-frequency phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) has shown potential as an effective EEG feature for 
ASSC. PAC is typically quantified using diverse metrics, such as envelope-to-signal (ESC), Modulation Index 
(MI), and cross-frequency coherence (CFC) 29–31. Specifically, ESC measures the correlation between the low-
frequency signal and the high-frequency envelope, while MI is calculated as the mean of a composite complex 
signal formed by the low-frequency phase and high-frequency amplitude envelope. Additionally, CFC quantifies 
the coherence between the high-frequency energy and the raw low-frequency signal. In the physiological aspect, 
the phase of the delta wave in the prefrontal cortex synchronizes with the amplitude of the spindle wave in the 
thalamus during sleep32,33. Manasova et al.34 found that delta-alpha PAC, which implies synchronization of delta 
wave phase and sleep spindle wave amplitude, significantly increases in the N2 and N3 stages compared to other 
sleep stages. Based on this phenomenon, Dimitriadis et al. 34 employed the delta-alpha PAC, calculated using 
various metrics including MI, as one of the key features for ML-based ASSC, applying traditional classifiers such 
as k-nearest neighbor, the Naïve Bayesian, linear discriminant analysis, and support vector machines to achieve 
enhanced classification performance.
Given the considerable potential of PAC, incorporating PAC information into DL is expected to enhance 
the overall performance of ASSC. Nevertheless, PAC presents challenges in its direct application to DL-based 
classification. First, using calculated PAC features directly for DL-based classification may yield varying 
performances due to the diverse methods to calculate the PAC measures 36. Second, the process of computing 
PAC features may result in information loss, potentially omitting critical characteristics of the original raw EEG 
signals. To address these problems, Formoso et al. recently employed a parallel frequency-band DL architecture 
to automatically extract the PAC-related information without human intervention; however, this approach 
implicitly provides the network with phase information in a low-frequency signal. To more effectively capture 
PAC-related information, it is necessary to explicitly represent the low-frequency phase, thereby facilitating the 
utilization of phase information by the DL model.
In this study, we attempted to automatically extract PAC features using specially designed DL architecture 
incorporating the Hilbert transform. Specifically, we propose SleepPACNet, a novel CNN-based DL architecture 
for ASSC, which was designed to effectively extract PAC features by leveraging phase information revealed 
through the Hilbert transform from a prefrontal single-channel EEG signal. SleepPACNet consists of two 
parallel components: the conventional feature extraction module and PAC feature extraction module. To enable 
CNN in SleepPACNet to capture the PAC information, we employed Hilbert transform to reveal the phase 
information in low-frequency EEG signal37, and the low-frequency phase information was integrated with the 
high-frequency amplitude information. To validate the classification performance of SleepPACNet, a bipolar 
prefrontal single-channel EEG signal was generated from the Dreem Open Datasets (DOD)38. Furthermore, to 
show the potential contribution of the PAC feature extraction module in SleepPACNet in classifying sleep stages, 
we compared the classification performance of the SleepPACNet with that of the CNN model containing only 
the conventional feature extraction module as well as those of state-of-the-art DL models for ASSC. To the best

Overall, the contributions of our study are as follows:
• We propose a novel DL architecture in which Hilbert-transformed low-frequency EEG signal and high-fre -
quency EEG signal jointly pass through CNN layers to extract features that incorporate PAC information, un-

for ASSC as well as our model without the PAC feature extraction module.
Materials and methods
This section first provides a comprehensive overview of the experimental data utilized to assess the classification 
performance of DL models. Then, it presents the detailed architecture of SleepPACNet, including the function 
of each component, as depicted in Fig.  1. The detailed architectural specifications and hyperparameters 
of SleepPACNet are provided in Supplementary information to ensure the reproducibility of the study 
(Supplementary Table 1).
Experimental data
Previous research32,33 has demonstrated that single-channel EEG signals recorded from the prefrontal region 
can benefit significantly from incorporating PAC features. Additionally, for implementing portable EEG devices 
intended for home use, bipolar prefrontal EEG channels obtained from the forehead region are typically preferred 
due to lower noise levels and greater ease of measurement. Consequently, we aimed to utilize a bipolar prefrontal 
EEG signal recorded via a single bipolar channel (Fp1–Fp2). However, most publicly available EEG databases 
do not contain such bipolar EEG recordings. Although the DOD38 also lacks direct Fp1–Fp2 signals, it provides 
prefrontal and frontal EEG channels such as Fp1–F3, Fp2–F4, and F3–F4, as detailed in the Supplementary 
information. These available channels allow simulation of bipolar prefrontal EEG signals suitable for portable 
EEG devices. Accordingly, we generated a bipolar prefrontal EEG signal, Fp1–Fp2, by combining Fp1–F3, Fp2–
F4, and F3–F4 channels, as follows:
 Fp 1 − Fp 2= Fp 1 − F 3 − Fp 2 − F 4+ F 3 − F 4 (1)
The PSG data in the DOD were recorded at a sampling rate of 250 Hz from 25 healthy participants. Sleep stages 
were manually scored into W ake (W), N1, N2, N3, REM, and ‘Not-scored’ at 30-s intervals by trained experts 
in accordance with the AASM scoring guidelines. The transformed Fp1–Fp2 EEG signals were segmented into 
non-overlapping 30-s intervals to correspond with the identified sleep stages, with segments classified as ‘Not-
scored’ being excluded from subsequent analyses. Each segment was then down-sampled to 100  Hz prior to 
z-score normalization to enhance overall computational efficiency.
Architecture of proposed model
SleepPACNet is composed of two parallel modules: the PAC feature extraction module and conventional raw 
EEG feature extraction module. The PAC feature extraction module captures PAC information by leveraging 
the phase of low-frequency EEG signals in conjunction with the amplitude of high-frequency EEG signals. In 
contrast, the raw EEG feature extraction module is designed to extract both temporal and frequency-domain 
information from unprocessed EEG signals.

To extract PAC features from EEG signals, it is necessary to use both the amplitude information, which is 
inherently present in the raw EEG signal, and the phase information, which is not explicitly observable. In 
this paper, to extract the phase information from the low-frequency EEG signal, raw EEG signal was bandpass 
filtered using a 6th-order zero-phase Butterworth infinite impulse response (IIR) filter with passband of 0.5–
8  Hz, and then Hilbert transform was applied to the filtered EEG data. The high-frequency EEG signal was 
obtained by applying a 6th-order zero-phase Butterworth IIR filter with passband of 8–30 Hz. These frequency 
bands were selected to take into account the frequency ranges for delta waves (0.5–4 Hz) and spindle waves (12–
15 Hz). These two inputs were concatenated along the feature axis, allowing the CNN to explore the interaction 
between the phase of the low-frequency signal and the amplitude of the high-frequency signal. However, direct 
feature concatenation of the two inputs was not feasible, as the low-frequency signal was a complex value 
owing to the Hilbert transform. Therefore, feature concatenation was performed on the high-frequency signal 
with both the real part and imaginary part of the Hilbert-transformed low-frequency signal. As a result, two 
CNN input features were obtained: Fr , representing the real part, Fi, representing the imaginary part, where 
F r =( f1,f 2, ..., f T ) ∈ R2×T  and F i =( f1,f 2, ..., f T ) ∈ R2×T , where T represents the number of 
time points.
Figure 2 illustrates the detailed architecture used to extract PAC features from the obtained features Fr  and 
Fi. T o investigate the relationship between the phase of low-frequency components and the amplitude of high-
frequency components by integrating them, three procedures were conducted on the features Fr  and Fi. First, a 
2D CNN layer with a kernel size of (2 × 1) and stride size of (1 × 1) was employed for each feature to capture 
the simultaneous relationship between the phase of low frequency EEG and the amplitude of high frequency 
EEG. Second, the following 2D CNN layer with kernel size of 100, to account for the PAC cycle locked to the 
delta wave, was applied to each feature map that was obtained in the first step. The utilized CNN layers employed 
the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) function 39 as the activation function. Finally, to integrate the feature 
maps derived from Fr  and Fi, the real and imaginary parts of the extracted feature maps, denoted by ˆFr  and ˆFi, 
respectively, were reconstructed into complex-valued representations as F complex = ˆF r + iˆF i. The complex 
norm of the Fcomplex was then computed to obtain the integrated feature maps. Additionally, the dropout layer 
with a rate of 0.5, followed by a max pooling layer with a pool size of 4 and a stride of 2, was applied as the final 
step.

It was assumed that PAC features would augment the capacity of the deep learning network to classify raw 
EEG signals. Therefore, the network architecture for the extraction of raw EEG features was not modified from 
conventional architectures. To extract these features, we employed a structure analogous to that utilized in 
various previous studies on ASSC, as illustrated in Fig.  318,19,40,41. This module is composed of two parallel 
blocks and a subsequent block: a CNN-based temporal feature extraction block with narrow kernels, a CNN-
based frequency feature extraction block with wide kernels, and a refinement block. Each feature extraction 
block comprises identical components, including the first 1D CNN layer, followed by a max-pooling layer and a 
dropout layer with a rate of 0.5, the second 1D CNN layer, and the third 1D CNN layer, which is also followed by 
a max-pooling layer. These CNN layers used the GELU function as an activation function. The wide kernel size 
of the first 1D CNN layer was set to 200, considering the input signal’s sampling rate of 100. Similarly, the narrow 
kernel size of the first 1D CNN layer was set to 50. Subsequently, the feature maps obtained from each module 
were concatenated along the time axis. To filter unnecessary information from the concatenated feature maps, 
refinement was conducted. In the refinement block, the concatenated feature maps were fed into a dropout layer 
with a rate of 0.5, followed by two additional 1D CNN layers to reduce the number of parameters. The activation 
function employed in these CNN layers was the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function 42. Finally, an average 
pooling layer was applied to reduce the sample size. All these structures and hyperparameters were optimized by 
repeated trial-and-error executions.
 3. Classification module
To leverage both the information of PAC features and raw EEG features, the feature maps from the PAC feature 
extraction module and the raw EEG feature extraction module were concatenated along the temporal axis. For 
refinement, a 1D CNN layer was inserted, with a kernel size of 8 and a stride of 2, to extract feature maps 
that amalgamate information from both extraction modules. Subsequently, the integrated feature maps were 
flattened following their passage through a max pooling layer, with a pool size of 4 and a stride of 2, and were 
then subjected to a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5. After this process, two fully connected (FC) layers were 
employed to calculate the probabilities of EEG segments being classified into each sleep stage. The first FC layer 
employed the ReLU activation function, while the second FC layer utilized the softmax activation function, as 
shown in Fig. 1.

Model training
The leave-one-subject-out cross-validation method was employed to enhance the reliability of classification 
performance. To prevent overfitting, 24 participants were randomly divided into training and validation sets in a 
21:3 ratio, excluding one designated for the test set. In this procedure, segments from the same participant were 
assigned to the same set to avoid information leakage between participants.
In the training phase, 100 epochs and a batch size of 128 were applied. To optimize the weighted loss 
function, AdamW43 optimizer was employed considering its superior generalization capabilities compared to 
other optimizers. The parameters included 0.9 of β1, 0.999 of β2, and a weight decay of 1 × 10−6. The cosine 
annealing learning rate scheduler44 was employed to prevent the DL model from converging to a local minimum 
during the optimization process. The Initial learning rate was set to 1 × 10−3, with a decay step of 1400. The 
parameter Tmult, Mmult, α were set to 1, 1, and 1 × 10−3, respectively. All the training and classification 
processes of DL models were conducted using Python 3.9.12 version with NVIDIA Geforce RTX 4070 Ti Super.

Models for comparison with SleepPACNet
To evaluate the classification performance of SleepPACNet objectively, we compared its performance with 
that of other DL models. Classification performances of the previous ASSC models, including SleepFCN 18, 
DeepSleepNet41, and TinySleepNet20 were implemented to prove that SleepPACNet outperformed these ASSC 
models. All the tested DL models were optimized so that they could achieve the highest ASSC accuracy. To 
ensure a fair and rigorous comparison with SleepPACNet, experimental data using the bipolar prefrontal EEG 
signals, model training from scratch, and evaluation were consistently applied to the previous ASSC models.
Ablation studies
To evaluate the contribution of the PAC feature extraction module in SleepPACNet, a conventional CNN-based 
DL model, denoted as SleepCNN, was implemented by excluding the PAC feature extraction module from 
SleepPACNet. Furthermore, several architectural variants were tested to justify the design choices of SleepPACNet 
from two perspectives: the efficacy of PAC feature extraction and hyperparameter optimality. First, regarding the 
efficacy of PAC extraction, the importance of explicitly exposing phase information was evaluated by assessing 
the case without the Hilbert transform. Within the same perspective, to demonstrate that the proposed module 
extracts more adaptive features, a case was conducted replacing the module with hand-crafted PAC features, 
specifically the MI calculated from the predefined frequency bands used in this study. Additionally, to investigate 
the most effective structural approach for complex-valued features, the performance was compared against a 
complex-valued CNN (CV-CNN) architecture, which was implemented as an alternative to the parallel CNN 
structure independently processing real and imaginary parts. Second, to ensure hyperparameter optimality, the 
influence of frequency band selection was examined by applying narrow frequency bands that precisely target

Statistical analysis
To examine which sleep stages showed significant improvement in classification performance, F1-scores of 
SleepPACNet and SleepCNN were compared for each sleep stage. The normality of the F1-score distributions 
was assessed by confirming that the absolute values of skewness and kurtosis were below 2 and 7, respectively46. 
Since the F1-scores satisfied the normality assumption, a paired t-test was performed on the F1-scores for each 
sleep stage in SleepPACNet and SleepCNN. To address the multiple comparison issue, Bonferroni correction 
was applied by multiplying the probability by five, which corresponds to the number of sleep stages47.
Results
Model classification performance
Table 1 presents the confusion matrix and per-class performance metrics of SleepPACNet. The N2 and N3 stages 
exhibited superior classification performance compared to other sleep stages, with F1-scores exceeding 0.8. In 
contrast, a large number of epochs in the N1 stage were misclassified into other sleep stages, with the exception 
of the N3 stage. Table  2 compares the classification performance of SleepPACNet with that of previous ASSC 
models. Both overall performance and per-class F1-scores were highest in SleepPACNet compared to all previous 
DL-based ASSC models, achieving a classification accuracy of 75.7%. Detailed per-subject classification accuracy 
is provided in the Supplementary Information to further demonstrate the generalizability and robustness of 
SleepPACNet (Supplementary Fig. 1).
Ablation studies of SleepPACNet
Table 3 summarizes the results of the ablation studies, where SleepPACNet serves as the reference model. As 
shown in the table, SleepPACNet outperformed SleepCNN in both overall performance and per-class F1-scores, 
improving classification accuracy by more than 2.4% compared to when the PAC feature extraction module was 
excluded. Regarding the efficacy of the PAC extraction module, replacing this module with hand-crafted PAC 
exhibited the lowest accuracy of 71.2%, which was even lower than that of SleepCNN. In contrast, while the case 
without the Hilbert transform and the use of CV-CNN architecture yielded higher accuracy than SleepCNN, 
both cases resulted in lower overall performance compared to SleepPACNet. Specifically, the case of using CV-
CNN outperformed the case without the Hilbert transform in terms of overall performance. With respect to 
hyperparameter optimality, both the application of narrow frequency bands and the reduction of the dropout 
rate to 0.2 resulted in lower accuracy compared to original SleepPACNet.

Discussions
In this study, we developed SleepPACNet, a novel CNN-based model incorporating PAC information, and 
we evaluated the ASSC performance of SleepPACNet. Our findings indicate that SleepPACNet is capable of 
accurately classifying sleep stages utilizing bipolar prefrontal single-channel EEG, which is beneficial for 
capturing PAC information while maintaining superior classification performance. SleepPACNet demonstrated 
enhanced performance compared to other ASSC models, such as SleepCNN, SleepFCN, DeepSleepNet, and 
TinySleepNet. Although SleepPACNet did not show a statistically significant improvement in performance 
compared to SleepCNN during the N2 and N3 stages, it achieved robust classification performance with an 
F1-score exceeding 0.8, demonstrating its ability to accurately detect deep sleep. Additionally, a statistically 
significant improvement was observed during the REM stage, indicating that the extracted PAC features played 
a crucial role in distinguishing REM sleep patterns in a bipolar prefrontal single-channel EEG. While CNNs are 
proficient at extracting amplitude features from raw EEG signals, they face challenges in effectively capturing 
phase features. To address this issue, Kim et al. 48 introduced the HiRENet, which facilitates the extraction of 
phase features from raw EEG signals via the Hilbert transform, demonstrating an enhancement in classification 
performance within deep learning frameworks. To extract PAC features, the Hilbert transform was utilized to 
clarify the phase information associated with low-frequency EEG signals. The importance of PAC information

In particular, the results of the ablation studies provide strong evidence that the application of the Hilbert 
transform to reveal phase information for the automatic extraction of PAC features plays a critical role in 
enhancing performance. Among the ablation studies focused on the efficacy of the PAC extraction module, 
replacing this module with hand-crafted PAC resulted in the lowest performance. This indicates that the PAC 
extraction module enhances classification by automatically selecting the most suitable PAC features among 
various PAC metrics for sleep stage classification, rather than relying on predefined fixed metrics. Furthermore, 
the finding that the case without the Hilbert transform outperformed SleepCNN but remained inferior to 
SleepPACNet suggests that while the separation of frequency bands provides some performance improvement, 
the explicit exposure of phase information using the Hilbert transform makes a more substantial contribution 
to the classification performance. Finally, the slightly higher performance of SleepPACNet compared to the case 
using CV-CNN may reflect that our architecture is functionally sufficient to capture essential phase information. 
Consequently, the consistent superior performance of SleepPACNet across three ablation studies demonstrates 
that revealing phase information and automatically extracting PAC features is the optimal configuration for 
sleep stage classification.
Furthermore, the results of an ablation study on narrow frequency bands (e.g., 0.5–4 Hz for the low-frequency 
band and 12–15 Hz for the high-frequency band) exhibited lower classification performance compared with 
SleepPACNet, which utilizes 0.5–8 Hz for a low-frequency band and 8–30 Hz for a high-frequency band. The 
frequency bands of SleepPACNet were empirically selected to comprehensively capture the characteristic 
interactions of both NREM and REM sleep stages. The enhanced performance of SleepPACNet is attributed to 
the inclusion of broader frequency ranges, specifically a low-frequency band (0.5–8 Hz) that incorporates both 
the delta waves (0.5–4 Hz) prominent during NREM sleep and the theta waves (4–8 Hz), characteristic of REM 
sleep. Similarly, the high-frequency band (8–30 Hz) encompasses not only NREM sleep spindles (12–15 Hz) 
but also beta activity (15–30 Hz), which has been reported to interact with theta rhythms in the frontal lobe 
during REM sleep44. This broad frequency band may enable the CNN to automatically extract cross-frequency 
relationships from both NREM and REM sleeps, such as delta–spindle coupling during NREM and theta–beta 
interactions during REM.
SleepPACNet al.so outperformed previous ASSC models in classifying the sleep stages using a bipolar 
prefrontal single-channel EEG signal. In our study, other previous ASSC models exhibited lower accuracy 
compared to the earlier research, which may be attributed to the use of a bipolar prefrontal single-channel 
EEG signal. Most prior studies employing single-channel EEG for ASSC used electrode pairs including central 
electrodes, such as Fpz–Cz, C3–M2, and C4–M1, which are recommended by the R&K and AASM manuals as 
key channels where sleep EEG features, such as sleep spindles and K-complex, are prominently represented 7,8. 
Accordingly, several studies utilizing Fp1–Fp2 signals have consistently reported lower performances; for 
instance, Shambroom et al.41 showed that ASSC using Fp1–Fp2 signal exhibited lower performance compared 
to that using PSG, and Stepnowsky et al. 42 reported a classification accuracy of approximately 70% using Fp1–
Fp2 signals. Consequently, the lack of distinct EEG characteristics in the prefrontal regions inevitably leads 
many state-of-the-art ASSC models to underperform in this study compared to their results in previous 
studies. However, the employment of a bipolar prefrontal single-channel EEG signal for ASSC is important 
from a practical perspective, such as when using a portable EEG device during sleep at home. This approach 
helps reduce noise caused by hair 49 and enables the use of electronic skin for EEG measurement. Therefore,

Scored sleep stages are commonly used to assess sleep quality and facilitate in its improvement. Among 
these stages, the N3 stage is recognized as one of the most important, as a deficiency in N3 stage can lead to 
fatigue, waking up feeling tired, and cognitive decline 51,52. Consequently, many studies have been conducted 
to increase slow oscillations of the N3 stage during sleep through auditory 53,54 or electrical stimulation 55,56. 
However, indiscriminate stimulation can disrupt sleep rather than enhance it 54, underscoring the need for 
precise, closed-loop stimulation specifically targeting the N3 stage. To achieve this, an accurate ASSC model 
is essential for reliably identifying the N3 stage. SleepPACNet demonstrates strong potential in this regard, 
achieving an F1-score exceeding 0.8 for N3 stage detection. This capability marks a significant advancement in 
the commercialization of home-based stimulation systems, as discussed in relation to real-world applications.
Aside from sleep research, PAC features have been employed as important markers for traditional ML-based 
classification across various domains, including neuropsychiatry, motor imagery, and emotion recognition57–60. 
Furthermore, many studies in these fields have attempted to use PAC features in DL-based classification. For 
example, Li et al.61 classified epileptic brain activity using handcrafted complex-valued PAC features with a DL-
based approach utilizing a complex-valued CNN. However, handcrafted PAC features may not always represent 
the optimal PAC measure for each specific classification task, potentially limiting improvements in classification 
performance in previous studies. Therefore, SleepPACNet could provide an alternative approach to enhance 
classification performance in other fields, as it automatically extracts features.
Although the PAC features were successfully extracted using SleepPACNet, the precise role of these features 
in classifying sleep stages remains unclear due to the inherent nature of DL ’s black box systems. This limitation 
emphasizes the necessity of investigating the extracted PAC features in greater detail to uncover the underlying 
neurophysiological phenomena associated with PAC during different sleep stages. Therefore, to verify whether 
the learned features align with physiological expectations using explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods, 
we visualized the class activation maps using Grad-CAM. As shown in Fig.  7, the heatmaps showed that the 
model’s attention was concentrated at specific phases of delta waves, precisely where sleep spindle waveforms 
were clearly observed in NREM stages (N2 and N3). In the REM stage, the importance weights increased 
around the peaks of theta waves, coinciding with regions where beta activity was distinctly visible. These results 
confirm that SleepPACNet successfully learned the well-known delta-alpha coupling in NREM and theta-beta 
interaction in REM. Additionally, these XAI methods could further expand the application of PAC by enhancing 
our understanding of its mechanisms. For example, there have been some attempts to improve memory 
consolidation, which is well-known phenomenon related to PAC that occurs when people fall asleep through 
the use of transcranial electrical stimulation (tES)33,62,63. As more detailed insights into PAC parameters, such as

There are several primary limitations in our study. Firstly, the absence of an open database containing 
bipolar prefrontal EEG signals with PAC information necessitated the theoretical generation of these signals to 
approximate those obtained through direct measurement. However, it is important to acknowledge that minor 
discrepancies may arise due to potential noise interference. Secondly, the limited number of sleep stage samples 
available constrains the application of deep learning-based classification methods. Thirdly, SleepPACNet was 
evaluated using only DOD, which may limit the generalization ability of our findings to different populations 
and recording setups. Consequently, further validation of SleepPACNet using additional datasets or diverse 
recording environments, with larger sample sizes obtained directly from bipolar prefrontal single-channel 
EEG signals, would significantly strengthen the evidence for the applicability of SleepPACNet. Additionally, 
although SleepPACNet primarily relies on CNNs to extract frequency-specific features, this architecture may 
have limitations in modeling long-range temporal dependencies across consecutive epochs. Previous ASSC 
studies have demonstrated that hybrid architectures combining CNNs with explicit temporal modeling modules 
can significantly enhance decoding performance. For instance, DeepSleepNet 41 utilized Bidirectional LSTM 
(Bi-LSTM) to capture sequential patterns, and AttnSleepNet40 employed a multi-head attention mechanism to 
learn temporal context from sleep EEG signals. Additionally, AtSiftNet 65 has also demonstrated that attention 
mechanisms can enhance EEG classification performance by effectively capturing temporal information. 
Therefore, a promising future direction is to integrate these temporal modeling mechanisms (e.g., Self-Attention 
or Bi-LSTM) into the SleepPACNet framework to better capture contextual information across sleep stages.
Conclusion
We developed a novel CNN-based ASSC model, SleepPACNet, which is capable of automatically extracting PAC 
features to classify sleep stages using single-channel EEG. The PAC features were extracted using CNN through 
the concatenation of a Hilbert-transformed low-frequency EEG signal, which enhances the accessibility of phase 
information, and a high-frequency EEG signal. To evaluate the classification performance of SleepPACNet, we 
used the bipolar prefrontal single-channel EEG signal, Fp1–Fp2, as it provides valuable PAC information and 
offers high potential for practical applications. The classification performance of SleepPACNet outperformed 
that of SleepCNN, indicating that the automatically extracted PAC features from SleepPACNet contribute to 
more accurate sleep stage classification.
However, the small sample size, the limited generalization arising from using a single dataset, and the use of 
generated signals, which may be affected by potential noise issues, may constrain our study. Future work should 
focus on improving classification performance and generalization by directly measuring bipolar prefrontal 
single-channel EEG signals from a larger number of participants across diverse populations or recording setups 
during sleep. Furthermore, the practical applicability of SleepPACNet can be further validated by employing 
portable EEG devices in subsequent studies. Additionally, the implementation of XAI methods in further 
studies could provide insights into the role of PAC features as biomarkers in classification, thereby broaden our 
understanding of the sleep phenomena associated with PAC.
